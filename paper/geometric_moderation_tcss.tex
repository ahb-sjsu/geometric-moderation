\begin{abstract}
Content moderation is usually cast as flat multi-label text classification.
That framing discards the social structure of the problem: a platform serves
many stakeholders whose values genuinely differ, and a single label can neither
represent nor govern that plurality. We reframe moderation as the construction
and governance of a \emph{per-stakeholder moral representation}. Each
stakeholder's values are encoded as an \emph{Ethics Module} (EM)---a weighting
over ethical-concern primitives that, composed in a directed acyclic graph
(DAG), the framework can enforce. EMs are constructed by interchangeable
methods: elicited through a structured game, fit from data, or authored. A
conversation is compiled---once per stakeholder ethos, via a structure-preserving
moral compiler---into a rank-4--6 moral tensor over the axes (dimension,
stakeholder, turn, action, coalition, uncertainty). The resulting per-stakeholder
verdicts are aggregated by an \emph{explicit} policy: \emph{worst-off} (the most
protective verdict governs, so a permissive stakeholder cannot override a harmed
one) and \emph{escalate} (cross-stakeholder disagreement, or any escalate-tier
verdict, routes to human review rather than collapsing to a confident scalar).
We additionally \emph{test, and report as a negative result}, the hypothesis
that embedding the policy taxonomy in hyperbolic space improves the underlying
classifier: on Civil Comments a flat baseline matches or beats a hyperbolic head
on macro-F1 and on cross-lingual verdict invariance. The contribution of
geometry to moderation is therefore not a better label but an auditable
per-stakeholder representation and the governance it enables. We release an
open-source reference implementation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ontent} moderation at platform scale is a computational social
system: automated classifiers, human reviewers, and written policy interact to
produce decisions that shape public discourse. The dominant technical framing
treats the automated component as flat multi-label classification---a
transformer encoder followed by a linear head and per-label sigmoids. This
framing is convenient and discards exactly the structure that makes moderation
hard as a \emph{social} problem.

The structure it discards is not primarily geometric; it is
\emph{stakeholder plurality}. A platform's communities, advertisers, vulnerable
users, regulators, and broader society do not share one value system, and the
disagreements among them are not noise to be averaged into a single score---they
are the substance of the moderation decision. A scalar toxicity probability
cannot represent ``this is permissible to a fairness-weighted community moderator
but harmful to a care-weighted vulnerable-user advocate,'' and a classifier that
silently picks one of those positions has made a contested metaethical choice
without disclosing it.

We therefore reframe the automated component. The object to compute is not a
label but a \emph{per-stakeholder moral representation of a conversation}, and
the job of the system is to \emph{enforce} the stakeholders' encoded values and
to \emph{surface}, rather than launder, their disagreement. Our contributions
are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A per-stakeholder moderation framework in which stakeholder values are
\emph{Ethics Modules} composed in a DAG, and a conversation is compiled---once
per ethos---into a rank-4--6 moral tensor (Section~\ref{sec:framework}).
\item A \emph{worst-off~$+$~escalate} enforcement rule that treats
cross-stakeholder disagreement as a first-class, non-aggregable escalation
signal (Section~\ref{sec:enforce}).
\item A cross-lingual \emph{Bond Index} evaluation of verdict invariance, with
the finding that a language-agnostic encoder---\emph{not} added hyperbolic
geometry---delivers the invariance (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item An honest \emph{negative result}: a hyperbolic policy-embedding classifier
does not improve over a flat baseline on macro-F1 or severity ranking on Civil
Comments, with an open-source reference implementation
(Sections~\ref{sec:impl}--\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Toxicity and hate-speech classification.} Public benchmarks---Jigsaw
Toxic Comment, Civil Comments \cite{civil}, HateXplain \cite{hatexplain}---have
driven strong flat classifiers. We reuse these benchmarks but change the
\emph{object} produced, from a label to a per-stakeholder representation.

\textbf{Value pluralism and participatory moderation.} A growing literature
argues that moderation must represent and govern conflicting community values
rather than impose one. We make this operational: stakeholder values are
explicit modules, and their conflicts drive escalation by construction.

\textbf{Computational and pluralist ethics.} A line of work compiles
natural-language moral material into structured representations evaluated under
multiple normative frameworks. We use such a compiler \cite{erisml} as the
substrate: a conversation is compiled per stakeholder ethos into a DEME moral
tensor, and disagreement across stakeholders is treated as a non-aggregable
signal.

\textbf{Hyperbolic representation learning.} Hyperbolic spaces embed trees with
exponentially less distortion than Euclidean space \cite{nickel}, motivating
their use for hierarchical labels. We tested this for the policy taxonomy and
report a negative result (Section~\ref{sec:eval}); it is included for
falsifiability, not as a contribution.

\section{The Per-Stakeholder Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Stakeholders as Ethics Modules}
A stakeholder's values are an \emph{Ethics Module} (EM): a weighting over ten
ethical-concern primitives---harm, autonomy, fairness, rights, care, fidelity,
legitimacy, epistemic quality, externality, repair---each projecting onto one of
nine moral dimensions. EMs are \emph{constructed} by interchangeable methods:
\emph{elicited} through a structured game (an advice-column ``Dear Ethicist''
probe that recovers a player's deontic structure---obligation, claim, privilege,
power---while they believe they are only giving advice), \emph{fit from data}
(e.g.\ the Social-Chem-101--derived \texttt{dear\_abby} and \texttt{aita} ethos
profiles used below), or \emph{authored}. The EMs are wired into a DAG whose
topology \emph{is} the moral composition, evaluated in dependency order; the
framework then enforces whatever the EMs encode. This is DEME:
democratically-governed ethics modules.

\subsection{A conversation as a moral tensor}
A thread compiles into a moral tensor of rank 4--6 over the axes
$(k,n,\tau,a,c,s)$: ethical \emph{dimension} $k$, \emph{stakeholder} $n$,
\emph{turn} $\tau$, \emph{action} $a$, \emph{coalition} $c$, and \emph{fact
uncertainty} $s$ (Monte-Carlo). ``Evaluate per stakeholder'' is literal: the
same conversation is compiled \emph{once per stakeholder ethos}, yielding that
stakeholder's slice of the $n$ axis together with its own verdict, confidence,
and a hash-chained decision proof (the profile hash plus a decision identifier)
for auditability.

\section{Enforcement: Worst-Off and Escalate}
\label{sec:enforce}
Different stakeholders, evaluated on identical content, return different
verdicts. In our reference implementation a harm/care-weighted ethos
(\texttt{dear\_abby}) returns \texttt{requires\_human\_review} on a
medical-confidentiality dilemma while a fairness-weighted ethos (\texttt{aita})
returns \texttt{permitted}. That divergence is not noise; it \emph{is} the
moderation event. The platform aggregates the per-stakeholder verdicts under an
explicit, restrictiveness-ordered policy (Algorithm~\ref{alg:wo}):
\textbf{worst-off}---the decision takes the most protective verdict---and
\textbf{escalate}---cross-stakeholder disagreement, or any escalate-tier verdict
(\texttt{requires\_human\_review}, \texttt{escalate},
\texttt{tragic\_conflict\_escalate}), routes to a human. This is the
operational form of a no-aggregation principle: the metaethical choice of how to
weigh stakeholders is surfaced and handed to a person, not smuggled into a hidden
weighting.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Worst-off $+$ escalate aggregation}
\label{alg:wo}
\begin{algorithmic}
\STATE \textbf{input:} thread $x$; stakeholder ethos set $\{e_n\}$; rank $r$
\FOR{each ethos $e_n$}
  \STATE $v_n \gets \textsc{CompileVerdict}(x, e_n, r)$ \COMMENT{per-stakeholder tensor + verdict}
\ENDFOR
\STATE $w \gets \arg\max_n \textsc{Restrictiveness}(v_n)$ \COMMENT{worst-off}
\STATE $\textit{disagree} \gets |\{v_n\}| > 1$
\STATE $\textit{tier} \gets \exists\,n: v_n \in \textsc{EscalateTier}$
\IF{$\textit{disagree} \lor \textit{tier}$}
  \STATE \textbf{return} \texttt{ESCALATE\_TO\_HUMAN}, witness $w$
\ELSE
  \STATE \textbf{return} $v_w$ \COMMENT{most-protective verdict governs}
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Distributional fairness as a proposed audit (not yet run)}
Because the framework yields per-stakeholder, per-dimension scores, fairness can
be audited distributionally rather than as a scalar gap: form each group's
score-covariance $\Sigma_g$ (a symmetric positive-definite matrix) and compare
groups by distance on the SPD manifold, exposing differences in the \emph{shape}
of behavior across groups, not only their means. We define this primitive
(\texttt{geomod.fairness}) but did \emph{not} run it in the present study; we
flag it as future work rather than report estimated numbers.

\section{A Tested Hypothesis: Hyperbolic Policy Embeddings}
\label{sec:hyp}
It is natural to ask whether the \emph{classifier} feeding the representation
benefits from hyperbolic geometry, since policy taxonomies are trees and
hyperbolic space embeds trees with low distortion \cite{nickel}. We embed the
policy tree in the Poincar\'e ball with the benign root at the origin and radius
growing with depth, compute logits as negative geodesic distances to policy
nodes, and read severity as (normalized) geodesic distance from the origin so
that classification and severity share one geometry. We treat this as a
hypothesis to be tested against a flat baseline, not as an assumed improvement;
Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports the outcome.

\section{Reference Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
The system is released as \texttt{geometric-moderation}. Its core,
\texttt{app/deme\_moderation.py}, implements Algorithm~\ref{alg:wo}: it compiles
a conversation once per stakeholder ethos through the ErisML compiler
\cite{erisml} (\texttt{eris-compile \dots\ --ethos-profile \dots\ --rank r}),
extracts each ethos's DEME verdict and decision proof, and returns an
audit-bearing decision (per-stakeholder verdicts, the worst-off witness, and
whether the case escalated). The repository also retains the exploratory
hyperbolic classifier and flat/hybrid baselines (\texttt{geomod}), the SPD
fairness primitive, and a FastAPI/browser visualizer; the serving app is
explicit when run in untrained demonstration mode.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Does hyperbolic geometry help the classifier? (No.)}
We run a four-configuration ablation on Civil Comments (20k train / 4k
validation, 7 active policy classes, DeBERTa-v3-base encoder, 3 epochs) after
fixing the geometric head's numerical issues (fp32 head over Poincar\'e ops;
gradient-safe norms; severity-by-radius). Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the
result. The flat baseline has the best macro-F1 by a wide margin
($0.500$ vs.\ $0.288$ for the hyperbolic head and $0.137$ for the full
geometric model); top-1 accuracy is dominated by the majority (benign) class and
is essentially tied. Severity rank-correlation with the ordinal label is weak
even where positive ($\rho=0.16$). This is a clear negative result for the
hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:hyp}: hyperbolic geometry does not improve
classification or severity on this benchmark.

\subsection{Cross-lingual verdict invariance (Bond Index)}
A separate claim is that geometry buys \emph{invariance}: the same content,
expressed in different languages, should receive the same verdict. We test this
with a frozen, language-agnostic encoder (LaBSE), training a flat linear head and
a hyperbolic head on English, then translating the English validation set into
Spanish, French, German, and Chinese (NLLB) and measuring the fraction of
translations that preserve the English verdict (the Bond Index $\beta$).
Table~\ref{tab:bond} shows the flat head is \emph{at least as} invariant as the
hyperbolic head ($\beta=0.997$ vs.\ $0.983$ mean-agreement; $0.997$ vs.\ $0.973$
all-languages-agree). The invariance is carried by the language-agnostic
embedding, not by the decision-surface geometry.

\subsection{Per-stakeholder enforcement (qualitative)}
On a medical-confidentiality dilemma the two shipped ethos diverge
(\texttt{dear\_abby}~$\to$~\texttt{requires\_human\_review};
\texttt{aita}~$\to$~\texttt{permitted}), and Algorithm~\ref{alg:wo} returns
\texttt{ESCALATE\_TO\_HUMAN} with \texttt{dear\_abby} as the worst-off witness.
On a borderline incivility thread both ethos return
\texttt{requires\_human\_review} and the case escalates on the tier rule. The
point of these cases is not accuracy on a label but that genuine value
disagreement is preserved and routed to a human, with a per-stakeholder audit
trail---behavior no flat classifier provides at any accuracy.

\section{Limitations}
The implementation is research-grade. (i) No production-validated checkpoint is
shipped; serving defaults to an explicit demonstration mode. (ii) The shipped
stakeholder set uses two data-fit ethos profiles as stand-ins; real deployments
construct stakeholder EMs by elicitation or authoring and drop them in as
additional profiles. (iii) The escalation substrate uses a rule-based extractor
for real-time use; richer LLM extraction is slower and optional. (iv) The
SPD-fairness and MRI primitives are defined but not empirically evaluated here.
(v) The negative result on hyperbolic geometry is established on Civil Comments;
we do not claim it generalizes to every taxonomy. These bound the empirical
claims, which is why every reported number is taken strictly from released runs.

\section{Conclusion}
Moderation should be modeled as a per-stakeholder moral representation that the
platform governs, not as a flat label. Encoding stakeholder values as Ethics
Modules, compiling a conversation into a rank-4--6 moral tensor once per ethos,
and enforcing \emph{worst-off~$+$~escalate} makes value disagreement a
first-class, auditable, non-aggregable signal. We also report, honestly, that
the tempting hyperbolic-geometry shortcut for the classifier does \emph{not}
improve accuracy or cross-lingual invariance over a flat baseline: the value of
the approach is the representation and governance, not the label. The
open-source reference implementation makes both the framework and the negative
result reproducible.
