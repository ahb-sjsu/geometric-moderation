\begin{abstract}
Content moderation is usually cast as flat multi-label text classification.
That framing discards the social structure of the problem: a platform serves
many stakeholders whose values genuinely differ, and a single label can neither
represent nor govern that plurality. We reframe moderation as the construction
and governance of a \emph{per-stakeholder moral representation}. Each
stakeholder's values are encoded as an \emph{Ethics Module} (EM)---a weighting
over ethical-concern primitives that, composed in a directed acyclic graph
(DAG), the framework can enforce. A conversation is compiled---once per
stakeholder ethos, via a structure-preserving moral compiler with an LLM
extractor---into a rank-4 moral tensor over the axes (dimension, stakeholder,
turn, action, coalition, uncertainty). Per-stakeholder verdicts are aggregated
by an \emph{explicit} policy: worst-off (the most protective verdict governs)
and escalate (cross-stakeholder disagreement, or any escalate-tier verdict,
routes to human review rather than collapsing to a confident scalar). We
evaluate the system on 240 real moral dilemmas (the AITA corpus) under two
shipped stakeholder ethos. Three findings: (i) the ethos encode systematically
\emph{different} value systems, in the direction their construction predicts
(e.g.\ the harm/care ethos weights physical harm significantly more negatively;
all per-dimension sign tests $p<0.001$; verdicts diverge on 34\% of posts);
(ii) the multi-dimensional tensor \emph{separates situation-level harm from
author-level normative violation}---aggregate harm does not track the human
culpability label ($\rho{=}{-}0.05$, n.s.), but specific normative dimensions
(fairness, care) do, structure a scalar toxicity score conflates; and (iii)
worst-off/escalate is consequential---relative to a single decisive
stakeholder, it routes 34\% more cases to human review by surfacing, rather
than laundering, stakeholder disagreement. We report honestly that DEME is not
a culpability classifier and that escalation does not discriminate among
uniformly hard dilemmas. We release the full evaluation and analysis harness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ontent} moderation at platform scale is a computational social
system: automated classifiers, human reviewers, and written policy interact to
produce decisions that shape public discourse. The dominant technical framing
treats the automated component as flat multi-label classification---a
transformer encoder followed by a linear head and per-label sigmoids. This
framing is convenient and discards exactly the structure that makes moderation
hard as a \emph{social} problem.

The structure it discards is \emph{stakeholder plurality}. A platform's
communities, advertisers, vulnerable users, regulators, and broader society do
not share one value system, and the disagreements among them are not noise to be
averaged into a single score---they are the substance of the moderation
decision. A scalar toxicity probability cannot represent ``this is permissible
to a fairness-weighted community moderator but harmful to a care-weighted
vulnerable-user advocate,'' and a classifier that silently picks one of those
positions has made a contested metaethical choice without disclosing it.

We therefore reframe the automated component. The object to compute is not a
label but a \emph{per-stakeholder moral representation of a conversation}, and
the job of the system is to \emph{enforce} the stakeholders' encoded values and
to \emph{surface}, rather than launder, their disagreement. Our contributions
are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A per-stakeholder moderation framework in which stakeholder values are
\emph{Ethics Modules} composed in a DAG, and a conversation is compiled---once
per ethos---into a rank-4 moral tensor (Section~\ref{sec:framework}).
\item A \emph{worst-off~$+$~escalate} enforcement rule that treats
cross-stakeholder disagreement as a first-class, non-aggregable escalation
signal (Section~\ref{sec:enforce}).
\item An empirical evaluation on 240 real moral dilemmas showing that the ethos
differentiate as designed, that the multi-dimensional tensor separates
situation-harm from author-violation, and that the enforcement rule is
consequential---together with honest negatives about what the system is not
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Toxicity and hate-speech classification.} Public benchmarks---Jigsaw
Toxic Comment, Civil Comments \cite{civil}, HateXplain \cite{hatexplain}---have
driven strong flat classifiers. We reuse the \emph{problem} but change the
\emph{object} produced, from a label to a per-stakeholder representation.

\textbf{Value pluralism and participatory moderation.} A growing literature
argues that moderation must represent and govern conflicting community values
rather than impose one. We make this operational: stakeholder values are
explicit modules, and their conflicts drive escalation by construction.

\textbf{Computational and pluralist ethics.} A line of work compiles
natural-language moral material into structured representations evaluated under
multiple normative frameworks \cite{erisml}, and learns norm/judgment models
from social data \cite{socialchem}. We use such a compiler as the substrate: a
conversation is compiled per stakeholder ethos into a DEME moral tensor, and
disagreement across stakeholders is treated as a non-aggregable signal.

\section{The Per-Stakeholder Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Stakeholders as Ethics Modules}
A stakeholder's values are an \emph{Ethics Module} (EM): a weighting over ten
ethical-concern primitives---harm, autonomy, fairness, rights, care, fidelity,
legitimacy, epistemic quality, externality, repair---each projecting onto one of
nine moral dimensions. EMs are \emph{constructed} by interchangeable methods:
elicited through a structured game, fit from data (the Social-Chem-101--derived
\texttt{dear\_abby} and \texttt{aita} ethos profiles used below
\cite{socialchem}), or authored. The EMs are wired into a DAG whose topology
\emph{is} the moral composition, evaluated in dependency order; the framework
then enforces whatever the EMs encode. This is DEME:
democratically-governed ethics modules.

\subsection{A conversation as a moral tensor}
A thread compiles into a moral tensor of rank 4--6 over the axes
$(k,n,\tau,a,c,s)$: ethical \emph{dimension} $k$, \emph{stakeholder} $n$,
\emph{turn} $\tau$, \emph{action} $a$, \emph{coalition} $c$, and \emph{fact
uncertainty} $s$. ``Evaluate per stakeholder'' is literal: the same conversation
is compiled \emph{once per stakeholder ethos}, yielding that stakeholder's slice
of the $n$ axis together with its own verdict, confidence, and a hash-chained
decision proof (the profile hash plus a decision identifier) for auditability.

\section{Enforcement: Worst-Off and Escalate}
\label{sec:enforce}
Different stakeholders, evaluated on identical content, return different
verdicts; that divergence is not noise, it \emph{is} the moderation event. The
platform aggregates the per-stakeholder verdicts under an explicit,
restrictiveness-ordered policy (Algorithm~\ref{alg:wo}): \textbf{worst-off}---the
decision takes the most protective verdict, so a permissive stakeholder cannot
override a harmed one---and \textbf{escalate}---cross-stakeholder disagreement,
or any escalate-tier verdict (\texttt{requires\_human\_review},
\texttt{tragic\_conflict\_escalate}, \texttt{indeterminate}), routes to a human.
This is the operational form of a no-aggregation principle: the metaethical
choice of how to weigh stakeholders is surfaced and handed to a person, not
smuggled into a hidden weighting.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Worst-off $+$ escalate aggregation}
\label{alg:wo}
\begin{algorithmic}
\STATE \textbf{input:} thread $x$; stakeholder ethos set $\{e_n\}$; rank $r$
\FOR{each ethos $e_n$}
  \STATE $v_n \gets \textsc{CompileVerdict}(x, e_n, r)$ \COMMENT{per-stakeholder tensor + verdict}
\ENDFOR
\STATE $w \gets \arg\max_n \textsc{Restrictiveness}(v_n)$ \COMMENT{worst-off}
\STATE $\textit{disagree} \gets |\{v_n\}| > 1$
\STATE $\textit{tier} \gets \exists\,n: v_n \in \textsc{EscalateTier}$
\IF{$\textit{disagree} \lor \textit{tier}$}
  \STATE \textbf{return} \texttt{ESCALATE\_TO\_HUMAN}, witness $w$
\ELSE
  \STATE \textbf{return} $v_w$ \COMMENT{most-protective verdict governs}
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Reference Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
The system is released as \texttt{geometric-moderation}. Its core,
\texttt{app/deme\_moderation.py}, implements Algorithm~\ref{alg:wo}: it compiles
a conversation once per stakeholder ethos through the ErisML compiler
\cite{erisml}, extracts each ethos's DEME verdict, the nine-dimensional moral
vector, and the decision proof, and returns an audit-bearing decision. Free-text
moral extraction uses a standard instruct LLM (\texttt{gpt-oss}) as the
extractor; the moral tensor, projections, and verdict are then computed
deterministically from the extracted facts. Because the extraction depends only
on the text and not on the ethos, it is computed once per post and reused across
stakeholders.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Setup}
We evaluate on the ``Am I the Asshole'' (AITA) corpus: first-person moral
dilemmas with a community verdict label (NTA = not at fault, NAH = no fault,
ESH = mutual fault, YTA = author at fault). AITA is the right input type for a
moral-scenario compiler---each post narrates actions, stakeholders, and
commitments---unlike one-line toxicity snippets, which carry no such structure
(the rule extractor degenerates to ``escalate'' on them; we therefore use the
LLM extractor throughout). We draw a balanced stratified sample of 60 posts per
class (240 total; 231 compiled successfully under both ethos), truncated to 1800
characters, and compile each under two shipped stakeholder ethos: a harm/care
advocate (\texttt{dear\_abby}) and a fairness/fidelity community
(\texttt{aita}), at tensor rank 4. We map the AITA label to an author-culpability
ordinal NTA$<$NAH$<$ESH$<$YTA. \emph{Circularity note:} the \texttt{aita} ethos
was fit on AITA-style judgments, so we lead with the \texttt{dear\_abby} ethos
(fit on a different advice column) for any label-referenced quantity.

\subsection{The ethos differentiate as designed}
Table~\ref{tab:diff} reports, per moral dimension, the signed mean difference
between the two ethos' values (\texttt{dear\_abby}$-$\texttt{aita}) and a sign
test over the posts where they differ. The differences are in the direction the
ethos' construction predicts and are highly significant: the harm/care ethos
weights \emph{physical harm} substantially more negatively (more harm-sensitive)
and \emph{care} more strongly, while the fairness/fidelity ethos weights
\emph{vow fidelity} more strongly. The two ethos return \emph{different}
categorical verdicts on $34.2\%$ of posts (mean inter-ethos moral-vector
distance $0.140$). Per-stakeholder modeling is therefore not vacuous: the
stakeholders' encoded values produce measurably, predictably different moral
representations of the same content.

\subsection{The tensor separates situation-harm from author-violation}
A scalar harm or toxicity score would have to answer ``how bad is this content''
with one number. The multi-dimensional tensor does not. Table~\ref{tab:valid}
correlates each dimension (under the primary \texttt{dear\_abby} ethos) with the
author-culpability ordinal. \emph{Aggregate} harm severity does \emph{not} track
culpability ($\rho{=}{-}0.05$, $p{=}0.41$)---and it should not: in NTA posts the
author is the \emph{victim}, so the situation contains high harm while the author
is not at fault. But specific \emph{normative} dimensions do track culpability:
fairness violation ($\rho{=}{-}0.18$, $p{=}0.006$), care ($\rho{=}{-}0.16$,
$p{=}0.014$), and autonomy ($\rho{=}{+}0.17$, $p{=}0.011$). These are nominal
($p<0.05$) under ten comparisons; only fairness survives a strict Bonferroni
threshold, so we report them as an exploratory but coherent pattern. The point is
structural: ``harm present in the scene'' and ``the author wronged someone'' are
\emph{different} quantities, separated by different tensor dimensions, and a
single harm scalar conflates them.

\subsection{Worst-off + escalate is consequential}
The enforcement rule is not cosmetic. Relative to the decisive harm/care
stakeholder acting alone, the joint worst-off/escalate policy reaches a
\emph{different} decision on $33.8\%$ of posts---routing them to human review
because the second stakeholder's uncertainty or disagreement is surfaced rather
than ignored. Escalation here is driven by two distinct ``do not silently
decide'' conditions: within-ethos indeterminacy on genuinely hard cases, and
cross-ethos verdict divergence ($34\%$). A single decisive stakeholder would
auto-decide a third of cases the multi-stakeholder system flags.

\subsection{Honest negatives}
Two claims a reader might expect, which the data do \emph{not} support, stated
plainly. \textbf{DEME is not a culpability classifier:} aggregate harm does not
predict the AITA verdict (above), because the system assesses the moral status
of a situation, not who is at fault. \textbf{Escalation does not discriminate
among hard dilemmas:} all $231$ posts exhibit cross-framework polarity
disagreement (every dilemma is, by the four frameworks' lights, genuinely
conflicted), so escalation fires almost universally ($91\%$) and does not
separate community-``contested'' (ESH/NAH, $91.2\%$) from ``clear'' (NTA/YTA,
$90.7\%$) posts. On a corpus selected for moral difficulty, ``route to a human''
is the right answer almost everywhere; the contribution is that the system
reaches it for principled, auditable, per-stakeholder reasons.

\section{Limitations}
The evaluation uses two shipped stakeholder ethos as stand-ins; real deployments
construct stakeholder EMs by elicitation or authoring and add them as profiles,
and a richer panel would sharpen the differentiation and governance measures.
The free-text extractor is an LLM, so extraction quality bounds the results; the
rule extractor is too weak for short or unstructured text. AITA supplies a
single author-culpability label rather than per-annotator vote splits, so our
contestedness operationalization is coarse. The per-dimension validity
correlations are exploratory under multiple comparisons. Results are on one
English corpus of deliberately hard dilemmas; generalization to ordinary
platform content is future work. Every reported number is reproduced by the
released harness.

\section{Conclusion}
Moderation should be modeled as a per-stakeholder moral representation that the
platform governs, not as a flat label. Encoding stakeholder values as Ethics
Modules, compiling a conversation into a rank-4 moral tensor once per ethos, and
enforcing worst-off~$+$~escalate makes value disagreement a first-class,
auditable, non-aggregable signal. Empirically, the ethos differentiate as
designed, the multi-dimensional tensor separates situation-harm from
author-violation in a way a scalar cannot, and the enforcement rule changes a
third of decisions---while we report honestly what the system is not. The
open-source harness makes every result reproducible.
